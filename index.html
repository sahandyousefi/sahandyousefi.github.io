About

AML Alert Intelligence

An end-to-end anti-money-laundering decision system that detects suspicious transfers, explains every alert, assigns investigators under capacity limits and drafts the Suspicious Activity Report, built to find more laundering with the same compliance team.

View the repositoryUpdated November 2026
Read the case studyClose the case study

The challenge

Banks raise far more anti-money-laundering alerts than investigators can review, and the large majority are false positives. Investigator hours go to noise while real laundering goes unreported.

A better risk score is only part of the answer. A compliance team also has to decide which alerts to work today, who should work them, and how to document each one for a Suspicious Activity Report (SAR), with an investigator accountable for every filing.

The solution

I designed alert handling as one decision system in four stages, and fixed the success metrics for each stage before training any model.

Detect. Graph neural networks (GraphSAGE, GAT and a temporal variant) that learn from how money moves between accounts, benchmarked against an XGBoost baseline on engineered features. Measured by PR-AUC, minority-class F1 and recall at the daily alert budget.

Explain. GNNExplainer and Captum for the graph models and SHAP for XGBoost, so every alert arrives with its evidence subgraph and key drivers, judged on fidelity and sparsity.

Prioritize. A mixed-integer program that assigns alerts to investigators to maximize expected illicit dollars caught under capacity, skill and SLA constraints, compared with greedy top-k and a fixed threshold under the same constraints.

Report. A small open-source language model that drafts SAR narratives from structured evidence. Fact precision is a hard gate: no number or entity in a draft that cannot be traced to the data.

Technical implementation

Data pipeline at scale. The raw CSV of 5.1 million transfers is parsed once in PySpark with an explicit schema and strict parsing, checked for row count and missing values, and stored as Parquet (127 MB instead of 476 MB). One YAML config sets paths, seed, split dates and Spark settings.

Evaluation that cannot leak. The split is chronological on whole calendar days: 3,248,921 transactions for training, 965,524 for validation and 863,900 for test. Because 147 of the 370 labeled laundering attempts cross a split boundary, no feature may memorize an account, and recall is also reported for attempts that start in the test period.

Point-in-time features. 46 features live in one registry, in three families: the transfer, both parties' account attributes, and each account's recent behavior. Behavior comes from a two-sided ledger built with Spark window functions: transfers and dollars in and out over 1, 24 and 72 hours, new counterparties, money flowing back between a pair (a sign of a cycle), and the share of a payment covered by what the sender received the day before. Every window closes one minute before the transfer. Amounts in 15 currencies are converted to US dollars with exchange rates recovered from the simulator's own cross-currency transfers on training days.

Exclusions on the record. The registry also lists what was left out and why: calendar dates (a hidden clock pointing at the end of the simulation), account and bank IDs (memorization), lifetime totals, an account's past laundering (label leakage) and round-amount flags (no signal). The feature dictionary is generated from the code, so the documentation cannot drift from it.

Tested like production code. 39 pytest unit tests on a shared Spark fixture include a check that future transfers cannot change past features and a check that the feature dictionary matches the code. The full feature table takes about 20 minutes to build on free Colab, so it is built once, saved, and refused at read time if it no longer matches the registry.

Results

Model results are not in yet: the XGBoost baseline is the next phase, and the repository reports nothing it has not measured. The data work has already changed how the models will be built and judged.

Found a simulation artifact. Normal traffic stops on September 10. The last eight days hold 1,108 transactions, 655 of them laundering, every one from an attempt still running when the simulation ended. They stay in the data, calendar dates are never used as features, and every test metric is reported with and without them.

Fixed an identity problem in the source data. 58 banks appear under two written IDs (such as 010 and 0010), which would have made 77 same-bank transfers look cross-bank and left no account matching the accounts file; 8 account numbers are also shared by two banks. Keying accounts on a normalized bank ID plus account number matches all 515,088 accounts.

Measured where the signal is. On the training days, ACH carries 83% of all laundering at 7.4 times the average rate, yet only about 1 in 173 ACH transfers is laundering, so payment format alone cannot separate cases. Among US dollar transfers, the median laundering amount is $4,833 against $971 for normal ones, and round amounts carry no signal.

Temporal consistency of fraud-model explanations

Research code behind my WPI FinTech Conference 2026 paper: a metric for whether a fraud model's SHAP explanations stay stable over time, compared across XGBoost, LSTM and Transformer models.

The challenge

A risk team that acts on model explanations needs them to be stable. If the features that drive a fraud model's decisions reorder themselves from one week to the next, the explanation is hard to defend to an analyst, an auditor or a regulator, even when accuracy holds.

Explanation quality is usually assessed on a single snapshot of data. That says nothing about stability over time, which is the property a production fraud model is actually judged on.

The solution

The Temporal Consistency Metric (TCM) measures that stability directly. The test period is cut into W consecutive windows. For each window, SHAP values are computed and averaged in absolute value per feature, giving one importance vector per window. TCM is the mean Spearman rank correlation between the importance vectors of consecutive windows. A score of 1 means the ranking of features does not change over time.

Because it only needs attributions, the same metric applies to any model family, which makes tree models and sequence models directly comparable.

Technical implementation

Three models are trained on a time-ordered sample of a public mobile-money transaction dataset with 15 features, nine of them engineered (balance changes, amount-to-balance ratio, emptied-origin and new-destination flags, transaction type risk, hour and day): XGBoost with class weighting, a two-layer LSTM and a Transformer encoder in PyTorch, both trained with a class-weighted loss.

Attributions come from SHAP's TreeExplainer for XGBoost and KernelExplainer for the neural models. The train and test split preserves time order.

Results

The sensitivity figure in the repository shows TCM above 0.98 for all three model families as the number of windows varies from 3 to 15, with scores declining slightly as windows get shorter.

The paper was published at the WPI FinTech Conference in October 2026.

Professional significance

It is original research on a question financial institutions care about: whether an explanation can be relied on over time, in addition to whether the model is accurate.

It connects to my production work, where SHAP explanations drive customer prioritization and revenue forecasts.

The challenge

Most sentiment work stops at the classification metric. In trading, a model can label headlines well and still produce a strategy that loses money once costs and turnover are counted.

The solution

FinSignal carries the analysis through to the number an investment team would care about: risk-adjusted return against a passive benchmark. A FinBERT model fine-tuned on the Financial PhraseBank produces a signed sentiment score per headline. Scores are aggregated by day, joined with price features (returns, rolling volatility, momentum) and fed in 10-day windows to a two-layer LSTM that predicts whether the next trading day closes up. The probabilities drive a long-or-flat strategy.

Technical implementation

The code is a small package with one module per stage (data, sentiment, signal model, backtest), dataclass configuration and command-line scripts for each step. The two models are trained independently so the sentiment encoder can be reused across tickers.

Leakage is handled explicitly: the scaler is fit on the training window only and the hold-out is strictly chronological. The backtest charges transaction costs whenever the position changes and reports total return, annualized Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown and hit rate for the strategy and the benchmark. Unit tests cover text cleaning, daily aggregation, feature construction, windowing and the backtest accounting.

Results

The repository publishes the pipeline and its tests, and reports no headline return. I do not claim one here. The README describes it as a research artifact and lists what real deployment would need: survivorship-bias-free data, slippage modeling, position sizing and testing across market regimes.

Professional significance

It shows the habit I bring to applied work: evaluate a model on the decision it feeds, with costs included, and state the limits of the result.

The code structure (typed configuration, separated stages, tests on the accounting) is what I aim for when a notebook has to become something others can run.

The challenge

In the PaySim data, the legacy rule-based flag has a correlation of 0.044 with actual fraud, and thousands of fraudulent transactions were never flagged. The task is a classifier that catches far more fraud without blocking too many legitimate payments.

The solution

Eleven features describe behavior that the raw columns miss, including whether an amount exceeds five times the sender's average, transaction counts and amount variability per sender and receiver, transfer and cash-out flags, hour of day and whether the destination is a merchant.

Missing a fraud costs more than reviewing a legitimate payment, so the decision threshold is lowered from 0.50 to 0.15 to favor recall. SHAP values are then used to confirm that predictions rest on interpretable signals.

Technical implementation

Legitimate transactions are randomly undersampled to a 10:1 ratio, and an XGBoost classifier (750 trees, depth 7) is trained on a stratified 80/20 split. Low-importance features, including the legacy flag, are pruned before the final fit.

Results

At the 0.15 threshold the model catches 1,407 of 1,643 fraudulent transactions in the test set (86% recall) and raises 790 false alarms among 16,426 legitimate ones, for 64% precision and an F1 of 0.73 on the fraud class.

These figures are measured on the undersampled test set. At the real fraud rate, precision would be lower; that is the natural next evaluation. SHAP shows amount, time step and transaction type as the main drivers.

Professional significance

It demonstrates the reasoning behind a fraud operating point: choose the threshold from the relative cost of the two errors, and check the model's drivers before trusting it. The threshold lab on this page lets you try the same trade-off.
